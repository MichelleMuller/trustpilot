# The US market — where Product Reviews should land

*Primary source: Trustpilot H1 2026 Results Call transcript ([`../reference/trustpilot-h1-2026-results-call-transcript.txt`](../reference/trustpilot-h1-2026-results-call-transcript.txt)) — verbatim, verifiable, quotable.*

---

## Why the US is the right place to point this

### 1. It's the biggest audience and the smallest business

- The **US is ~28–30% of trustpilot.com traffic** — the largest single market, ahead of the UK at ~18–20%
- But the revenue base is UK-led, and Blair put **UK market penetration at "still low at around 5%"**

If the *UK* — the mature market, the brand's home turf — is at 5%, the US is far below it. **The US has Trustpilot's largest consumer audience and its smallest commercial footprint.** That gap is the opportunity, and it is the single clearest place to point a new product line.

The CEO has quantified the prize elsewhere: at UK penetration levels, the US alone would be a **billion-dollar-plus ARR business**.

### 2. The flywheel is already spinning there faster than the monetisation

From the H1 call, North America:

| Metric | H1'26 |
|---|---|
| Bookings growth (cc) | **+27%** |
| New business sales | **+76% YoY** |
| **Organic reviews** | **+50% YoY** |
| Paying customers | +11% YoY |
| $20k+ customer growth | +43% YoY |

**Organic reviews +50% against paying customers +11%.** Supply is growing four times faster than monetised demand. There is already US review inventory arriving that Trustpilot isn't charging for — and product-level collection is the way to make more of it commercially useful.

New US enterprise logos in the half: **Bed Bath & Beyond, Intact Insurance, Sunrun**. ARR share from $20k+ customers in the US is *"approaching similar levels to the UK, which stands at 49%... albeit the US is coming from a much lower base."*

### 3. The US ecommerce market is where product reviews actually matter

- US ecommerce: **~$1.3–1.6 trillion** in 2026
- **2.7–3.5 million active** US online stores (13.6m registered)
- **~2.86 million live Shopify storefronts**; Shopify merchants captured **>14% of US ecommerce**, with **$216.3bn GMV in H1'26, +33% YoY**
- Amazon and Shopify together are now roughly **50% of US ecommerce**

And the consumer demand signal is strong:
- **95%** of consumers use reviews when shopping; **86%** say reviews are essential to deciding
- A product with **five reviews converts at ~270%** the rate of the identical product with none
- **71% of US consumers** say a good Trustpilot score makes them more likely to **buy**; **73%**, more likely to **trust**
- **57%** of Americans trust crowd-sourced review sites

### 4. The distribution rail already exists — Shopify

Trustpilot became a **Shopify Key Product Partner** in June 2026, with an upgraded **Shopify 2.0 app** that *"merchants can seamlessly embed Trustpilot directly within their native ecosystem"* — functioning as both merchant-facing collection tool and customer-facing showcase.

That is the single most important asset for a US product-reviews push, and it's underused. Shopify is ~14% of US ecommerce and the default platform for exactly the mid-market and emerging brands where product reviews are acute. **The rail is built; the product running on it is thin.**

## The uncomfortable part: Blair's framing cuts against an ecommerce-only thesis

From the H1 call, verbatim:

> "It's important to note that **product reviews are not just for retail**. Most businesses divide what they do up into different offerings. For example, a bank with different accounts, or **TUI, who are using product reviews for specific holiday offerings**."

This is a deliberate, stated broadening of Product Reviews beyond ecommerce SKUs to **"offerings"** — bank accounts, insurance products, holiday packages, course types, software tiers.

It matters for two reasons:

**It validates the strategy's direction but widens its scope.** Trustpilot's strongest US verticals are financial services, insurance, banking, credit unions, education, retail, software and hospitality — and the showcase customer in the H1 call is **Navy Federal Credit Union**, not a DTC brand. Product Reviews as "offering reviews" maps onto the enterprise verticals that are already working.

**It gives us the reconciliation we needed.** `07-practitioner-view.md` argued the acute need is mid-market ecommerce; `09` flagged that this conflicts with the CEO's enterprise focus. Blair's "not just for retail" framing resolves it:

> **Ecommerce is where product reviews are most acute and where the collection volume comes from. "Offerings" is how the same product sells into the enterprise verticals that are already winning.** One product, two go-to-market motions — the Shopify rail for ecommerce breadth, the vertical enterprise motion for depth.

That's a stronger position than choosing one, and it doesn't pick a fight with stated company strategy.

## What the CEO confirmed about *why* collection is the lever

Directly on how LLM citation is won — this is the clearest endorsement of a collection-first strategy in any source we have:

> "The consistent theme across all of that is that **relevance, recency, ranking** are the key things. So in other words, having a **large enough volume of reviews** such that the LLM can surface an answer using reviews that is exactly relevant to the user's query, then having reviews that are **recent**. So if your reviews are **stale and many months old, then it's much less likely to get cited** by a large language model... **the more feedback and the more frequent feedback companies are getting, the more likely they are to be showing up in large language model search.**"

**Volume, freshness and relevance — all three are collection problems.** The CEO has described the mechanism; the product doesn't yet exploit it. Nothing in the current offering makes recency targetable or freshness legible, and the submission form makes the relevance-bearing data (attributes) optional.

Also stated: Trustpilot's **domain authority is 100**.

## The attribution gap — acknowledged on the record

Asked directly where citation data comes from:

> "We look at external sources like Seer Interactive... Secondly... we look at **our own server logs**, which tell us every time we get a request from one of the large language models. Now those internal server logs **aren't a perfect representation of citations** because the models are training on our data. They're not just doing real-time inference... **the data we see in our server logs is a small subset of overall citations.**"

And asked whether they can help brands assess ROI:

> "We **are doing more** with customers in this area. You'll have seen an ROI study... with Forrester that showed a **401% ROI**... but we are doing more particularly with larger customers to help them really quantify the ROI. **It isn't of course as straightforward as simply things like traffic metrics.**"

**The measurement gap is real, acknowledged, and unsolved.** That's useful two ways: it confirms the attribution opportunity is genuine, and it warns that proposing it as a headline bet means proposing something already on the CEO's list. Better positioned as the *metrics layer* under the strategy than as a third bet.

On whether AEO converts commercially:

> "When we talk about 35% growth in customers paying more than 20K, **almost every conversation** we'll have had with those businesses **involves AEO at some level**... Is it supporting willingness to pay? **Absolutely.**... It's helping with our GRR."

- Sales reorganisation: outsourced top-of-funnel BDR, split customer success from account management

## The US thesis in one paragraph

> Trustpilot's biggest audience is in the US and its smallest business is in the US. Organic US reviews are growing 50% a year against 11% growth in paying customers, so the supply is already arriving unmonetised. The rail into US ecommerce — Shopify, ~14% of the market — is built and underused. Product Reviews is the product that converts that audience and that rail into revenue, because product-level reviews are what US ecommerce actually needs and what AI actually cites in the comparison queries that decide purchases. And because "products" means *offerings*, the same build sells into the financial services, insurance and travel verticals that are already Trustpilot's strongest US wins.
