# The CEO's stated strategy — Adrian Blair

*Primary source: full transcript at [`../reference/scaling-up-adrian-blair-transcript.md`](../reference/scaling-up-adrian-blair-transcript.md) — Scaling Up S10.E2, TDM Growth Partners, 29 Oct 2026. Cross-checked against the [H1 2026 results call transcript](../reference/trustpilot-h1-2026-results-call-transcript.txt).*
*All quotes below are verbatim and safe to use.*

This is the most important source in the repo. It tells us what the CEO already believes, which determines what we're *adding* versus what we're *arguing with*.

## 1. The founding insight is portable reputation — and that *is* our positioning

Peter Mühlmann, 2007, selling cables on eBay:

> "He built up a very good reputation, very good seller rating... wanted to start doing it, going it alone, selling it off the platform, and **realised he couldn't take his seller rating with him**, which is a bit of a problem. So what he realised is... the world needed something more like what we call a **universal symbol of trust**. So in other words, something that **didn't just exist on eBay, but existed in lots of other places as well.** And that was the genesis of Trustpilot."

**This is the single most valuable thing in the transcript for our deck.** Trustpilot exists because reputation couldn't travel. Our positioning — *be wherever the consumer makes the buying decision* — is not a new idea bolted onto the company. **It is the founding insight, restated for the AI era.**

Reputation that couldn't travel from eBay to your own shop is the same problem as product reputation that can't travel from your PDP into a ChatGPT answer or a Walmart product page. The deck can open here.

## 2. The mission, stated as a mandate for distribution

> "What we want to do is... provide the kind of **trust layer or trust infrastructure for AI**. So get trust signals like reviews **in very large quantity**, make them **as trusted as possible** and **get them into as many places as possible** where people are going to find them useful to decide for themselves who they can trust."

Three clauses, and they map exactly onto the strategy:

| Blair's clause | Our bet |
|---|---|
| "in very large quantity" | **Bet 1** — collection volume and coverage |
| "as trusted as possible" | **Trade-off 2** — no incentivised reviews, ever |
| "get them into as many places as possible" | **Bet 2 + Bet 3** — discovery, free widgets, uncapped invitations |

## 3. Version 3.0: trust signals and trust impact

> "One is **growing trust signals**, so getting more and more information that people or AI finds helpful in determining who they can trust. So that means reviews, obviously, but **we're interested in other trust signals as well**... So more trust signals and then **trust impact**. So helping consumers to actually make sense of the information, **helping AI agents to make sense of the information**."

And the flywheel:

> "The more impact it's having, the more reason there is for people to want more of these reviews of their businesses on the platform, the more they invite people to write reviews, and the whole thing just grows organically."

**Product attributes and sentiment are trust signals.** Bet 1 is literally the expansion he describes. And he names **AI agents** as a constituency to be served.

**New fact:** Trustpilot has **"opened up to Meta Muse"** — *"because we think Meta Muse will do a better job if it's using the feedback that's on Trustpilot."* Active surface expansion, not just passive crawling.

## 4. Open platform — and the explicit rejection of licensing

> "There was essentially two schools of thought... One was **let's lock it down, let's keep it proprietary, and then let's try to licence it out piecemeal and to the highest bidder**... The second approach was kind of the opposite, which is, **we're an open platform, we want to make the data as useful as possible to as many people as possible. And obviously I went for the second approach.**"

> "...that means **giving our data a life of its own well beyond the Trustpilot platform itself.** So ultimately, whether you are finding out about Trustpilot reviews through trustpilot.com or through ChatGPT or Claude, whatever it might be, **it's all good.**"

**This closes a question the repo had open.** `04-competitive-landscape.md` floated converting the uncontracted organic citation position into model-provider data deals, Bazaarvoice-style. **Blair considered exactly that and rejected it.** Do not propose it. The strategy is openness, and the deck should build on it rather than relitigate it.

Results he attributes to it: **ChatGPT citations up 400%**, and *"many external parties are now saying we're one of the **top 10 most cited domains on the planet**, right up there with folks like Wikipedia or YouTube."*

## 5. Neutrality — stated more tightly than I'd assumed

> "Trustpilot is **not a marketplace**, it's **not any kind of retailer**. So we have **no vested interest in whether a transaction happens at all**, let alone which provider a transaction happens with. So we're **not a transactional service** and **we don't monetise at all through engagement metrics**. So we're **not an advertising business either.**"

Four exclusions, all binding on Bet 2:
1. Not a marketplace
2. No vested interest in transactions
3. **No monetisation through engagement metrics**
4. Not advertising

The third is the one I'd missed and it's the sharpest. A consumer product-discovery surface cannot be justified on engagement, traffic or attention economics. It has to earn its place by making the *subscription* more valuable. See Trade-off 3 in the deck.

Plus the architectural commitments: a business *"can't stop you"* writing a review, *"can't come to us and say please delete that review"*, *"can't even say to us, please take our profile away."*

## 6. The moats, in his words

**Data and fraud detection:** 400m+ reviews, 200,000+ new reviews daily. *"The systems are probabilistic. It's not like a court of law... It's more like spam detection in email or fraud detection with credit card companies... spotting the digital fingerprints, spotting the patterns."*

**Brand, and why cross-industry beats vertical:**
> "You have these kind of vertical industry review services... TripAdvisor... Yelp... you can't possibly remember a different review service for every single one of those industries. What is needed... is a universal symbol of trust that just covers all of those industries... **there just isn't that much room in people's heads for dozens more brands that represent trust.**"

**The three-way combination:**
> "A massive addressable market... a fantastic business model... **83% gross margins**... and thirdly, **there's no obvious comparator**."

## 7. The business model is fixed — and it isn't quite SaaS

> "B2B subscription. **Not quite SaaS in the sense that it's not like the workflows inside companies, it's more about the data and the infrastructure**, the trust in the edge of AI. But the business model is that businesses subscribe to that for a period of time, **typically a year to three years**."

**Useful framing for Bet 3.** If the product is data and infrastructure rather than workflow software, then charging per seat, per invitation and per widget is the wrong shape. Infrastructure is priced on value delivered, not on metered inputs. That's an argument for the repackaging, in his own terms.

## 8. Enterprise focus — and the reasoning behind it

> "We're going to emphasise particularly **the larger businesses** where we just see such a huge need for C-level teams to just more deeply understand trust."

The analogy he uses is the sales pitch:
> "Think about money and how well a typical CEO or CFO understands money. You have a whole team devoted... your P&L, your balance sheet, your cash flow... But then you say to a CEO, 'What's the most important thing?' And they'll actually say before we get onto all the money stuff, you've got to have trust. **Well, how well do you understand that? Is there a team? Do you get reporting on it?**"

> "We saw this **amazing product market fit at the high end**."

**Four focus markets:** UK (~40% of business), US (~20%), Germany, Italy. Paying customers in 100+ countries.

**His own three priorities on arrival:** strategic clarity → great execution → profitability.

## 9. The US

> "We grew 27% in the US... We grew new business in the US by some of that 76% year-on-year."

> "Vinny Petrillo, our leader in the US and his team... focusing on certain industries, particularly where we just have amazing product market fit. Right across **financial services**, for example, whether that's **insurance, banking, folks like credit unions** or **wellbeing** or **education** or **certain areas of retail**, **software**, **hospitality**."

Offices in **New York and Denver**. He is *"spending a lot of time in the US these days."*

> "If we were at the scale in the US relative to the economy that we already are in the UK, **we would already be a billion dollar plus ARR business just in the US.**"

⚠️ **Note the phrasing: "certain areas of retail."** Retail is listed fifth, hedged, and behind financial services. **The US success story is financial-services-led, not ecommerce-led.** See the reconciliation below.

## 10. On AI internally

> "You always had this trade-off in business where you couldn't have all three of **quality, cost and speed**... I think AI is doing this thing now where you can have **all three of those things at once.**"

AI review summaries are already live on high-volume profiles, and in business products for insight extraction. Engineers use Claude Code. Values: *"We start with the customer"*, *"We make it happen"*, *"We win together"*.

# What this means for our strategy

## Where we align — and can borrow his exact words

| Our position | His words |
|---|---|
| Be wherever the buying decision happens | *"Something that didn't just exist on eBay, but existed in lots of other places as well"* — **the founding insight** |
| Distribution of trust to the point of decision | *"Get them into as many places as possible where people are going to find them useful"* |
| Richer attributes beyond star ratings | *"We're interested in **other trust signals** as well"* |
| Serving AI agents, not just consumers | *"Helping **AI agents** to make sense of the information"* |
| Conversational collection at low cost | *"Quality, cost and speed... you can have all three"* |
| Free widgets, uncapped invitations | *"Make the data as useful as possible to as many people as possible"* |
| Repricing away from metered inputs | *"Not quite SaaS... it's more about **the data and the infrastructure**"* |
| No incentivised reviews | Reviews removed only if proven fake; *"not in the pocket of businesses"* |

**Present the strategy as executing his stated vision at product level.** That's a far stronger interview position than proposing a different direction.

## Where we must be careful

### 1. Segment — retail is *not* the lead US vertical

He lists *"certain areas of retail"* fifth, after financial services, insurance, banking, credit unions, wellbeing and education. The H1 showcase customer is **Navy Federal Credit Union**.

Our practitioner view says product reviews are most acute for mid-market ecommerce. **Both are true, and the H1 call gives the reconciliation in his own words:**

> *"Product reviews are **not just for retail**. Most businesses divide what they do up into different offerings. For example, **a bank with different accounts**, or **TUI, who are using product reviews for specific holiday offerings**."*

> **The resolution: "products" means *offerings*. Ecommerce is where collection volume and product-intent query demand are richest. Offerings is how the same product monetises into the financial services, insurance, travel and education verticals already winning in the US. One build, two go-to-market motions.**

This is stronger than picking a side, and it avoids contradicting stated strategy on slide four.

### 2. Neutrality — the engagement-monetisation exclusion binds Bet 2

*"We don't monetise at all through engagement metrics. So we're not an advertising business either."*

A consumer product-discovery surface **cannot** be justified on traffic, attention or engagement. It must earn its place by making the business subscription more valuable — more citation, more collection, better data. Build that argument explicitly, with the guardrails in Trade-off 3.

### 3. Do not propose model-provider licensing

He considered *"licence it out piecemeal and to the highest bidder"* and rejected it on mission grounds. Proposing it would read as not having listened.

### 4. The constructive gap — open platform vs. what robots.txt does

He says *"get them into as many places as possible"* and *"make the data as useful as possible to as many people as possible."*

`08-prp-live-investigation.md` found `/product-reviews/` disallowed to **every** crawler — Googlebot, OAI-SearchBot, GPTBot, PerplexityBot, ClaudeBot — plus no product sitemap and no consumer product search.

**That is the gap between stated strategy and shipped product, in the CEO's own words.** Not a criticism — the cheapest, most on-strategy fix available. *(Still needs one confirmation.)*
