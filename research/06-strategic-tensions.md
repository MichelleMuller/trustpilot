# Strategic tensions — the questions the deck has to answer

Research doesn't produce a strategy; choices do. These are the real forks. Each one has a defensible answer in both directions — which is what makes them the content of the deck rather than background to it.

---

## Tension 1 — Serve the agent, or serve the human?

The brief names both: *help consumers confidently evaluate products* **and** *help businesses drive visibility in LLMs*.

| Serve the agent | Serve the human |
|---|---|
| Machine-legible infrastructure: feeds, APIs, schema, attribute data, licensing deals | A consumer product-discovery destination on top of 361m reviews |
| Offence into a growing channel | Defence of a declining one (58% of traffic is organic search) |
| Monetises immediately — businesses pay for visibility | Monetises indirectly — but it's what generates the reviews in the first place |
| Risk: becoming invisible plumbing with no brand and no pricing power | Risk: investing in a surface consumers increasingly bypass |

**The knot:** agents value Trustpilot because humans wrote the reviews. Humans write reviews because the destination exists. Starve the destination and the asset decays; over-fund it and you're defending a channel AI is eating.

**Most likely right answer:** these are not 50/50. One is the bet and the other is the maintenance budget. The deck has to say which — and say it in one sentence.

---

## Tension 2 — Destination or distribution?

Trustpilot's PRP launch is a **destination** play: better pages on trustpilot.com, independently indexed, waiting to be crawled. Bazaarvoice's play is **distribution**: 1,750+ retailer PDPs plus a contracted feed into Google's Shopping Graph.

- Destination preserves independence, brand and the consumer relationship — and depends on crawling continuing to work the way it does today
- Distribution puts the content where decisions happen — and gives up the direct relationship

**The uncomfortable fact:** 99.5% of Trustpilot's citations are organic. Not one of them is contracted. The entire AI-visibility proposition rests on model providers continuing to choose Trustpilot voluntarily. Gemini already cites it at 21% versus 49–58% elsewhere, and that's the engine where a competitor has a deal.

**Question for the deck:** should Trustpilot pursue direct data partnerships with model providers (OpenAI, Google, Perplexity, Anthropic) — and what does it give up if it does? Licensing review data converts an uncontracted organic position into contracted revenue, but risks commoditising the thing businesses currently pay for.

## Tension 3 — Enterprise upsell or SMB land-grab?

The white paper's central finding is a **step function**: 1% → 54% at ~13 reviews. The biggest value is created at the *lowest* threshold of activity.

The business model points the other way: Product Reviews is a paid add-on on upper tiers; the >$20k cohort is 45% of ARR and growing 36% YoY; AACV is rising deliberately as Trustpilot focuses on higher-value enterprise customers.

**The contradiction:** the product's most powerful proof point is about how cheap and fast the first step is, while the product itself is priced for companies that have already taken it.

| Enterprise | SMB |
|---|---|
| Where the ARR and the growth are | Where the Seer data says the value is created |
| Co-mention effect (9.5x) justifies ongoing spend | Presence cliff (50x) is a one-time land |
| Defensible against Profound only by bundling measurement + remedy | Loses on price and features to Judge.me ($0–15) and Okendo |
| Sales-led, slow, high ACV | Product-led, fast, low ACV — and the April 2026 **$99 Starter plan** is the vehicle |

**Note:** the co-mention finding is the genuinely strong enterprise argument — direct visibility plateaus, competitive visibility keeps scaling with volume. That's a recurring-revenue logic, not a one-time land. If the enterprise bet is right, co-mention share is the metric it hangs on.

---

## Tension 4 — Is the premise even true?

The brief asserts Product Reviews is "uniquely positioned." Interrogate it:

**Evidence for:** #1 cited review platform on three of four engines; independent third-party validation is the one thing a brand cannot self-supply via a merchant feed; holds both measurement and remedy; 361m reviews and real fraud infrastructure.

**Evidence against:** every headline stat in the white paper measures **company** profiles, not product reviews. The product-level evidence is a beta-cohort >1,500% citation lift off a near-zero base and a 12 ppt brand citation lift. No retailer syndication. Weaker product-level depth than ecommerce-native tools. Study ran March 2026; the landscape moved materially by November.

**The strongest version of the answer probably says:** *Trustpilot is uniquely positioned at the **company** level — that's proven at scale. At the **product** level it is not yet uniquely positioned; it is uniquely **able** to become so. The 12–18 month job is to convert a company-level moat into a product-level one before Bazaarvoice converts a distribution network into an AI one.*

That reframe is defensible, evidence-based, and more interesting than accepting the premise.

---

## Tension 5 — Citation is a proxy. What happens when buyers notice?

Nothing in the study connects a citation to a click, a consideration, a conversion or a dollar. Right now the market accepts citation rate because there's nothing better. That will not hold for 18 months.

Enterprise buyers renewing a $20k+ contract will ask: *what did the citations do?*

- Trustpilot already observes that **AI-sourced visitors arrive with unusually high buying intent** — that's the start of the chain, and it is unexploited
- AI-sourced traffic to US retail sites grew **393% YoY** in Q1 2026
- Nobody — not Profound, not Bazaarvoice, not Trustpilot — has closed the loop from citation to revenue

**This is the biggest unclaimed position in the category.** Whoever proves "AI citation → purchase" owns the budget conversation, sets the pricing model, and makes citation-rate dashboards into a commodity. It is also the natural answer to the assignment's third criterion, which asks precisely how you would *know the strategy is working*.

---

## Tension 6 — Does scaling product reviews put the moat at risk?

Trustpilot's entire value to an LLM is that its content is **trustworthy and independent**. Scaling product-level review collection means more volume, more SKUs, more incentive to game, and more surface area for fraud.

- Fake review submissions rose from **8% to 10%** of all submissions YoY; 3.9m removed in H1'26 alone
- Product-level reviews are a softer target than company reviews — more SKUs, lower per-item scrutiny, direct commercial payoff per manipulated item
- Trustpilot's fraud infrastructure is a genuine competitive asset and a cost centre that scales with volume

**If model providers ever lose confidence in review platform integrity, the citation rate goes to zero and the entire strategy with it.** Any growth initiative in this space carries a trust-infrastructure line item. That isn't a compliance footnote — in this strategy it's a core investment thesis, and arguably the most defensible thing to spend on.

---

## The trade-off the deck must name explicitly

The assignment asks "how would you handle the trade-offs?" — which means naming what is **not** being done. Candidates for explicit sacrifice:

- **Not** competing with Judge.me/Okendo on SMB product-review features or price
- **Not** building a general-purpose AEO measurement suite to compete with Profound head-on
- **Not** building a retailer syndication network from scratch to match Bazaarvoice's 1,750 sites
- **Not** building a full consumer shopping/comparison destination
- **Not** chasing non-English locales before the English-language product-level position is proven

A strategy that sacrifices nothing isn't a strategy. The deck should say "no" to at least two of these out loud.

## Addendum — revisions from the practitioner view

`07-practitioner-view.md` (Michelle's first-hand Yotpo experience and a teardown of the live product review flow) materially revises three of the tensions above:

- **Tensions 1 and 2 collapse into a single positioning.** *"Trustpilot has already won the destination; the job now is to be wherever the consumer makes the buying decision — Walmart or ChatGPT."* Retailer syndication and LLM citation are two surfaces of one strategy, not competing bets. Product review volume is the inventory that unlocks both.
- **Tension 2's real content is query divergence, not channel choice.** Company-intent queries ("is this a scam") and product-intent queries ("best X for Y", "does this run small") are different queries on both ChatGPT and Google. Trustpilot answers the first better than anyone and the second barely at all — and the second is larger, closer to purchase, and where the 9.5x co-mention effect actually plays out. Company-level reviews still carry real merchant-trust value on a PDP; they just can't drive SKU-level conversion alone. The **pairing** — independent answers to both questions from one source — is something no competitor holds.
- **Tension 3 has a sharper edge than "where's the money."** Trustpilot's enterprise commercial motion and its Product Reviews product are aimed at *different customers*. The acute need sits with mid-market and emerging ecommerce — the segment Trustpilot currently prices out.
- **A fourth consideration the desk research missed entirely:** the weakest link is **collection**, not visibility. And Trustpilot holds an unmeasured structural advantage there — invitations sent from trustpilot.com, with better deliverability and higher consumer response than a competitor sending from a brand's own domain.
